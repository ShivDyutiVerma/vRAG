## Surprises & learnings

- A result that looks solid under a careful local test can still be wrong about the thing that
  actually matters live, and the gap between them can be genuinely informative rather than just
  "bad luck." Steady-state RSS after a request completes and peak RSS during a cold singleton's
  first real initialization are different measurements — the local test was real and correctly
  performed, it just answered a slightly different question than the one that mattered.
- Checking runtime logs directly, rather than inferring from symptoms, turned an ambiguous "it's
  returning 502s, probably OOM?" into a confirmed, specific diagnosis (no access-log line before
  each restart) in a couple of minutes. Worth defaulting to "get the actual logs" over "form a
  plausible theory" whenever the tooling makes it this cheap to just check.
- `git revert` instead of force-pushing or manually redeploying an old SHA was the right call for
  a real reason, not just convention: a straight "redeploy the old commit" would have left the
  branch itself still pointing at the broken code, so the very next ordinary push (mine or R's)
  would have silently re-broken the demo. A revert commit makes the rollback part of the actual
  history everyone's working from, not a side-channel fix that only lives in Render's dashboard.
- Trying something and getting a real, live, negative answer was more valuable this session than
  continuing to project or debate what the answer might be — it converted an open-ended "maybe
  646MB is enough headroom, maybe not" question into a specific, actionable fact (peak load is the
  real constraint, not steady-state), at the cost of a few minutes of live downtime that was fully
  planned for and recovered from cleanly.
